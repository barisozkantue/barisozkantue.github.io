Research Methods Guide

How to Prepare an MSc Research Proposal

A good proposal balances what you want to find out with what you can realistically do, given your data, time, resources and methods. This guide walks through the six elements every proposal needs and the storyline that connects them.

📄 6 elements 🔁 Iterative by design 🎯 MSc thesis research
1
Title One line
Name what the reader will find
"Does my title signal the goal and context of my research?"

The title is the first promise you make. It should point to the goal or context of your study and make a reader want to know more, telling them at a glance what your thesis delivers.

A good title does two things

Signals the goal or context of your research
Arouses interest and indicates what the reader will find in your thesis

Avoid

Generic themes or sub-discipline names, such as "Process Modeling in Organization XYZ". They name a field, not a study.
2
Problem context Short section
Build the case for your research
"What does the reader need to know to see why this research matters?"

Set the stage so the significance of your work becomes self-evident. Move from why the topic matters, to the problem that exists, to what current knowledge misses. End with a clear statement of the gap or need: this and that, therefore there is a need for …

Four rules for this section

Ground it in literature. Link your arguments to articles wherever possible.
Stay focused. Leave out dimensions and references that add nothing to your argument.
Keep it company-independent. The context must hold completely without referring to your project company.
Keep it short. Background and related literature get their own chapter in the thesis.
3
Significance Own section
Position your contribution
"Where does my research fit, and what will it add?"

Given the gap, explain where your study fits and how it differs from or complements existing work. Focus on the scientific contribution, and state it as one or more research goals or questions, adding sub-goals or sub-questions where they help.

Decide what kind of research this is

Prescriptive research: your main goal is to develop or (re)design something, as in design science research.
Explanatory or descriptive research: you explore and investigate a phenomenon of the empirical world.

Test

Every goal or question must be directly and explicitly answered by your research outputs. If an output can't answer it, rewrite the goal.
4
Objective Own section
Name your concrete outputs
"What will exist at the end of my study?"

You have said what you propose and why. Now say what you will deliver. Objectives are tangible research outputs, not purpose statements, and each one should follow from a goal or question in Step 3.

Outputs can be

Design artifacts: a model, framework, method, technique, tool, product or algorithm
Findings about the phenomenon: success factors, challenges, evaluation results
A mix of both: for example a framework together with the results of evaluating it
5
Research design Research Method section
Choose your approach, methods and techniques
"How will I answer my questions with the data, time and resources I have?"

Following your research questions, choose a research approach, then the methods and techniques within it. This is where the balance is tested: your design has to fit the data you can get, its quantity and quality, and your time and resources.

Typical choices

Approaches: design science research methodology, case study, grounded theory, Delphi study
Data collection and reviews: interviews, surveys, controlled experiments, simulation, systematic or narrative literature review
Analysis techniques: ANOVA, classification, decision trees, integer programming

Tip

Link each method or technique to the roadmap step where you will use it (Step 6).
6
Roadmap Section + figure
Sequence the work
"Which tasks will I do, and in what order?"

Break the research into work packages and show their logical order, since one step often needs the outputs of another. A literature review typically comes first.

In the Research Roadmap section

A figure showing your steps and their dependencies
The method used in each step, matching your research design
Optionally, a Gantt chart with milestones and deadlines

A living document

Expect your proposal to change after the research starts: an assumption turns out false, data is unavailable, a better method appears, new literature is published, or the company's expectations shift. When one element changes, review all the others so they stay logically connected as one storyline.

The proposal does not need to be perfect. It needs to be compact and to show clearly how your research problem and your method relate. It is good enough when it tells you what to do and why:

What do I do next? Why am I doing this?

A proposal that covers all six elements is easy to discuss with your supervisors, gives them a structure for organized feedback, and becomes the blueprint of your thesis.

Three things that weaken a proposal

✗

Goals written as wishes. Research goals and questions are not generic purpose statements. If no output of your study answers them explicitly, they are too vague.

✗

Objectives that restate the purpose. An objective names something that will exist at the end: a model, a tool, a list of success factors, evaluation results.

✗

A context padded with broad literature. References to very broad topics add length, not argument. Keep only what leads the reader to your gap.
